## Executive Summary

**The thesis in one sentence.** Orbital power for orbital consumers (compute, manufacturing, lasers, beam-propulsion) is launch-cost-dominant; Starship working makes the bet pay; Starship plateauing reduces the helio chain to the Phase 1 reflector-services market.

**What goes wrong with the space-based-solar-power-to-ground proposals.** Every serious SBSP-to-ground proposal — NASA OTPS, Mankins SPS-ALPHA — assumes the point is to beam electricity from space to Earth via microwave or laser to terrestrial rectennas. That assumption is the dominant economic trap. (The recent orbital-compute entrants — Suncatcher, Starcloud, Starcloud's customers — already reject ground delivery; they avoid this trap rather than fall into it, and the helio chain sits with them on that point.) NASA's 2024 OTPS report demonstrates it brutally — even at $1,000/kg launch costs, SBSP delivered to ground runs $0.61/kWh (Mankins SPS-ALPHA-class) or $1.59/kWh (Tethered-SPS), 12-80× more expensive than terrestrial renewables (§1.2). The 8-stage end-to-end conversion chain (PV cell 35% × DC-DC 90% × DC-to-RF 70% × antenna emission 90% × atmospheric transmission 98% × beam collection 95% × rectenna reception 78% × ground DC-DC 90% ≈ 13.0%) destroys more than half the orbital power before it reaches a terrestrial customer. The helio chain explicitly rejects this architecture.

**What the helio chain actually is.** All electricity generated in orbit stays in orbit, consumed by orbital data centers (hybrid GPU/TPU hardware running compute-as-a-service when external customers buy, Bitcoin mining when idle), in-space manufacturing (Varda-class customers), laser-based beam propulsion for Mars cargo, and (eventually) high-energy-physics infrastructure. The conversion chain rises from ~13% to **~24%** because the 4 ground-delivery stages disappear (v0.11 efficiency-tightening; was 31.5% in v0.10). The right cost comparison is not terrestrial renewables — it is **Suncatcher-class self-launched-PV at $0.0925/kWh** (per Suncatcher §2.4 at $200/kg launch).

**The keystone result, direct from the v0.8 model with v0.11 efficiency-tightening.** At projected $200/kg launch costs (Suncatcher's published target, requiring sustained 20% Starship learning rate through ~1,800 additional launches and 370K additional tonnage cumulatively), orbital-power cost delivered to orbital consumers is **$0.0091/kWh** — roughly 10× below the Suncatcher self-launched-PV baseline (§2.4). At current $3,600/kg, it is **$0.1635/kWh**. The single most-sensitive parameter is launch cost; nothing else matters as much.

**Reflected sunlight to ground IS in scope — but as photons, not generated power.** Phase 1 reflector services (premium illumination per mission + thermal warming + greenhouse photons + PV augmentation) deliver natural sunlight redirected by thin-film aluminized-mylar mirrors. **Premium illumination per mission** was falsification-tested 2026-05-25 (5/5 scenarios CLOSE) and then physics-deliverable-audited 2026-05-26 (v0.12). The audit caught that a single 100 kg / 614 m² satellite delivers ~1 lux average over a ~24 km² spot — moonlight-comparable intensity, not useful for defense ISR / SAR / disaster pricing tiers. The corrected verdict: **single-satellite premium illumination closes at SPECTACLE pricing tier only** ($5K-$50K per event); defense-grade deliverables require either Reflect-Orbital-style constellation coordination OR ~10-tonne single satellites. **Thermal warming** was similarly RETRACTED in v0.11 after a thermodynamics engineering audit caught a 230× per-pass dimensional overstatement; physics-corrected falsification fails at single-satellite scale. **Phase 1 launch-cost-tolerance currently rests on single-satellite spectacle-tier premium illumination alone** — a meaningfully narrower TAM than the v0.10-v0.11 framing implied, but a real one validated by Reflect Orbital's commercial traction at adjacent pricing tiers (50K-mirror constellation target by 2030; USAF $1.25M contract; 260K customer applications).

**Phase sequence.** Phase 1 (reflector services to ground) starts now and is launch-cost-tolerant. Phase 2 (orbital compute — both compute-as-a-service mode AND Bitcoin mining self-buyer mode on the same hybrid hardware fleet, with the BTC fallback eliminating the utilization-floor problem that plagues terrestrial DCs) requires launch cost ≤$500/kg. Phase 3 (in-space laser beam propulsion to Mars cargo) requires ≤$200/kg plus demonstrated 1+ MW beaming. Phase 4 (high-energy-physics infrastructure in orbit; not Earth-receiving) requires the entire stack. Forward-looking Phase 3-4 funding option: helio-chain-native cryptocurrency / tokens representing stake in the space colonization mission (regulatory structuring pending; §5.9 #17).

**Downside protection** comes from the four-wedge research-portfolio structure. If orbital compute never closes even at $50/kg launch, the helio chain still has Phase 1 reflector services to ground (launch-cost-tolerant for premium illumination at spectacle pricing tier; defense-grade and thermal warming are constellation-dependent per v0.11-v0.12 audits), beam propulsion (depends on Mars cargo market existing), and orbital-power-as-a-service to other orbital customers (Varda-class). The kill case is not "shelve the project" — it is "lean harder on the Phase 1 spectacle revenue and treat orbital compute + constellation buildout as future-upside."

**Honest uncertainty surface.** §5.9 enumerates 17 open questions spanning launch-cost trajectory beyond Starship V1, Bitcoin halving / price-volatility risk + GPU-vs-ASIC mining efficiency, helio-chain-native token regulatory structuring, thermal warming physics-scaling, astronomy / public-trust legitimacy for visible orbital infrastructure overhead, ground-segment economics, and others. The article does not predict whether the four assumed curves (launch cost / compute cost / AI capability per watt / robotics + in-space manufacturing) will continue to bend; it documents the conditions under which they would, and the consequences if they do not.

## 0. Why this article exists

In 1993, the Soviet space program's successor — Russia's RKK Energia — unfurled a 20-meter aluminized-mylar mirror in low Earth orbit.[^11] It spun gently to maintain its shape via centrifugal force, then directed a 5-kilometer-wide spot of reflected sunlight across the predawn Atlantic and into Europe, moving at 8 kilometers per second. The illumination was about as bright as a full moon. Most ground observers, hampered by clouds and the brief transit time, didn't see it. Russia called the satellite *Znamya* — "Banner." Six years later they tried to do it again at larger scale, the deployment mechanism snagged on a rendezvous antenna, the foil tore, and the project was abandoned.

That experiment is the historical anchor for the entire orbital-power infrastructure debate. The physics works. The engineering is hard. The economics, depending on whose model you trust, are either trivially obvious or laughably impossible. And every couple of years a startup or a paper or a national-lab report relitigates the same set of questions — usually without showing the math, almost never showing the math in a form you could argue with.

Most orbital-power articles are written from one of two postures. The first is starry-eyed advocacy: launch costs are falling, solar is abundant in space, therefore the future is obviously gigawatt orbital arrays beaming clean power down to Earth. The second is reflexive dismissal: the math has never worked, the engineering is still impossible, therefore the whole idea is forever fantasy.

Both postures share a defect. Neither one has a model that anyone can run. **Both also share an assumption — that the point of orbital power is to deliver electricity back to Earth via microwave or laser beams to ground rectennas.** That assumption is precisely what makes the economics fail, because the ground-delivery chain (8 stages of conversion from solar cell to ground DC) yields only ~13% end-to-end efficiency (NASA OTPS, see section 1.2) — and the comparison to terrestrial renewables at $0.02-0.05/kWh is brutal at any plausible launch cost.

This article takes a different approach. The helio chain explicitly rejects the SBSP-to-ground premise. **All electricity generated in orbit is consumed in orbit** — by orbital compute, in-space manufacturing, laser-based beam propulsion to Mars cargo, and (eventually) high-energy-physics infrastructure. The relevant cost comparison is NOT helio chain $/kWh vs terrestrial renewable $/kWh; it is helio chain $/kWh-delivered-to-orbital-consumer vs Suncatcher-class $/kWh-from-self-launched-integrated-PV. The 8-stage SBSP chain shortens to a ~24%-efficient orbital-only chain (PV cell × DC-DC plus realistic orbital loss stages per the v0.11 audit; the bare 2-stage PV×DC-DC figure was 31.5%) when only the orbital portion is needed.

Reflected SUNLIGHT to ground IS in scope — but as photons, not as generated electricity. Premium illumination per mission (defense ISR / SAR / disaster events), PV augmentation (reflected sunlight onto terrestrial PV farms à la Reflect Orbital), greenhouse photons (high-latitude commercial growers), and thermal warming (airports / mining towns / Arctic bases / eventually cold-city winter pay-per-service) are all reflector-sunlight architectures, not power-beaming architectures. They share the helio chain's reflector infrastructure but bypass the ground-delivery efficiency penalty entirely.

I built a parametric economics model — 12 inputs, 4 output classes, sensitivity sweeps, stalled-progress scenarios — and asked: under what specific conditions does the helio chain (orbital-power-for-orbital-consumers + reflector services to ground) generate net-positive revenue? When does it fail? Which of the underlying tech-curves (launch cost, compute cost, AI capability per watt, robotics and in-space manufacturing) must bend favorably for the economics to close?

The model is the keystone. The verbal argument is downstream of it.

Here is the punchline up front:

> **At projected mid-2030s conditions (launch $200/kg, mature in-space manufacturing, all four cost/capability curves bending per assumption), the cost of orbital power delivered to orbital consumers (compute, manufacturing, lasers) is $0.0091/kWh — roughly 10× below the $810/kW/yr Suncatcher-class self-launched-PV baseline ($0.0925/kWh). If launch cost plateaus at today's $3,600/kg, orbital power cost rises to $0.1635/kWh — comparable to current Starlink-class launched-power-price economics ($14,700/kW/yr ≈ $1.68/kWh) and above the Suncatcher baseline. Both numbers are direct outputs of the v0.8 `orbital_power_cost()` model function with v0.11 efficiency tightening (24% orbital-only chain incorporating MPPT + distribution + eclipse + BOL/EOL + temperature derating per engineering audit). The single most-sensitive parameter is launch cost; nothing else matters as much.**

A sharper, more honest restatement: **$500/kg launch cost is necessary for orbital compute to close net-positive on multiple workload classes; $1,500/kg is not enough.** This matters because the *midpoint* of plausible launch-cost outcomes over the next decade — not the worst case, the middle case — is closer to $1,500/kg than to $500/kg (the model has no priors or distributions over launch outcomes; "midpoint" here is the arithmetic centerpoint of the bounds, not a statistical median — see section 5.6 for the same caveat). The helio chain bets that we end up in the *lower half* of the plausible launch-cost range, not the midpoint. That is a meaningful bet, not a foregone conclusion.

A second honest finding sharpens the bet further: **the midpoint scenario — every model input pinned to the arithmetic centerpoint of its plausible range — is uniformly uneconomic across all five orbital-compute workload classes**, with orbital-power LCOE landing well above the Suncatcher self-launched-PV baseline. The orbital-compute wedge only closes in the *lower half* of the joint distribution. Three other wedge categories — reflector services (premium illumination; thermal warming retracted 2026-05-26 per R2 — see §5), beam propulsion, and orbital-power-as-a-service to other orbital customers — close at higher launch costs and are not dependent on the compute break-even, which is why the article's four-wedge structure matters (section 2.2 and section 5.6).

This is the central finding. Everything else in the article is either (a) the traps that have killed prior orbital-power proposals, and how the helio-chain framing avoids them; (b) the phase-by-phase build plan with tech-readiness gates rather than calendar dates; (c) the regulatory exposure that nobody plans for; (d) what specifically fails if any one of the four assumed curves stalls; or (e) the kill case — what we do if compute economics never close, even at $50/kg launch cost.

If you want the model, run it: `python helio_chain_economics.py`. If you want to argue with the assumptions, every numeric input carries a tier flag (T1 = direction-setting only, T5 = empirically reproduced); the placeholder ranges are explicit; the source memos are public.

### 0.2 What surprised me running the model

Five surprises shaped the v0.7-v0.12 version of the article:

*The first surprise: the SBSP-to-ground premise itself was the wrong frame.* I started this project assuming the helio chain was, like the classic space-based solar power proposals (NASA OTPS, Mankins SPS-ALPHA), ultimately about delivering electricity to Earth — and I wrongly lumped the orbital-compute efforts (Suncatcher, Starcloud) in with them, when those in fact already keep the power in orbit. The parametric economics model I built around that assumption produced the kind of LCOE numbers that have killed every prior SBSP proposal: $0.017/kWh at $200/kg launch and $0.30/kWh at current launch costs, against a terrestrial-renewables comparison that ranges $0.02-0.05/kWh. The numbers were defensible — they were direct outputs of NASA's 8-stage conversion chain — and they made the helio chain barely competitive at the most optimistic launch-cost projection, with no margin for adversarial review. Several drafts later, the model still kept telling me the same thing: SBSP-to-ground doesn't close cleanly. It took an explicit scope clarification to realize the premise was wrong, not the parameters. The helio chain is orbital power for orbital consumers; it never needs to compete with terrestrial renewables because it never delivers to Earth. The 8-stage ~13%-efficient chain shrinks to a ~24%-efficient orbital-only chain, the LCOE drops to $0.0091/kWh, and the comparison anchor moves from terrestrial renewables to Suncatcher self-launched-PV. The numbers stop fighting the thesis and start supporting it. The lesson: when a model keeps telling you something doesn't work, sometimes you're modelling the wrong product.

*The second surprise: Phase 1 reflector services to ground are launch-cost-tolerant TODAY.* I expected the entire helio chain to be gated on Starship maturation — that is the explicit "bet on the four curves" framing the article uses. Then the 2026-05-25 premium-illumination falsification ran (§3.1, footnote [^17]). A 100 kg thin-film reflector satellite in LEO sun-sync orbit, delivering short-duration illumination of named ground targets, closes net-positive at current $3,600/kg launch under ALL FIVE tested scenarios — from a worst-case Reflect-Orbital-baseline 20 missions/yr × $50K (net +$597K/yr) through to an aggressive defense-ISR-heavy 150 missions/yr × $1.5M (net +$225M/yr). The wedge does not need to wait for Starship. This means the helio chain has a year-one commercial revenue path that is independent of the launch-cost-bend thesis. The bet on Starship maturation gates Phase 2+ (orbital compute / manufacturing / beam-prop) but not Phase 1. The article is sharper for distinguishing these.

*The third surprise: the midpoint scenario is uniformly uneconomic.* When I ran a sensitivity analysis pinning every input to the arithmetic midpoint of its plausible range — not the worst case, not the best, just the boring middle — I expected to find a soft-but-survivable case. Instead, the midpoint scenario produces an orbital-power cost of $0.1177/kWh, well above the Suncatcher self-launched-PV baseline of $0.0925/kWh. Across all five orbital-compute workload classes, the midpoint case shows uniform loss. The orbital-compute wedge only closes in the *lower half* of the joint launch-cost distribution. This is the kind of finding that makes the bet feel more honest: the helio chain isn't betting on "average" outcomes; it is betting on a specifically optimistic set of outcomes for launch cost. The four-wedge research-portfolio structure exists precisely because the midpoint scenario does not close on a single-wedge bet (§5.6).

*The fourth surprise (v0.11): I had thermal warming wrong.* The 2026-05-26 morning thermal-warming falsification appeared to validate the wedge across 11 customer-class/pricing scenarios at $3,600/kg launch. An afternoon engineering audit caught what I'd missed: the Solspace 35 MWh/pass anchor I used describes a constellation of large mirrors, not a single 100 kg satellite. A 100 kg / 614 m² satellite physically intercepts only ~0.84 MW of solar flux and can deliver ~0.15 MWh thermal per overpass — about 230× less than I'd assumed. The physics-corrected re-run fails all 11 scenarios at all tested launch costs. The honest reframe: thermal warming is a Phase 2-3 research-portfolio item requiring constellation-scale economics, not a Phase 1 launch-cost-tolerant entry point.

*The fifth surprise (v0.12): premium illumination had the same dimensional issue, just less catastrophic.* The same audit logic — what does a 100 kg / 614 m² satellite actually deliver to ground? — applied to premium illumination too. The math: ~1 lux average over a 24 km² spot at 600 km altitude. That's moonlight intensity — useful for atmospheric / cinematic spectacle (~10 lux threshold) but NOT for defense ISR / SAR / disaster ops / construction (all need 50-200+ lux). The 2026-05-25 unit-economics falsification used $50K-$5M-per-mission pricing tiers that assumed defense-grade deliverables a single satellite cannot physically provide. **Physics-corrected re-run at spectacle pricing ($1K-$50K per mission): 5 of 6 scenarios still CLOSE.** The wedge survives — just at narrower TAM than originally claimed. Higher-WTP customer classes require constellation coordination (Reflect Orbital's 50K-mirror playbook) or 10-tonne single satellites. The lesson: the engineering audit caught two related dimensional errors in two days, both rooted in the same aperture limit. Tier discipline + falsification framework didn't catch them; physics-deliverable audit did. v0.12 is materially more honest than v0.11.

These five surprises shaped the v0.7-v0.12 article's central architecture. Now let us start with the traps.

## 1. The dominant trap (and four supporting traps) orbital-power concepts fall into

But one of the five is dominant — it determines whether the economics can EVER close, regardless of how the others move. The article structures §1 accordingly: §1.1 is the dominant trap (the SBSP-to-ground product premise itself); §1.2-§1.5 are four supporting traps that interact with the dominant one. ### 1.1 The dominant trap: framing the product as electricity delivered to ground

This is the trap. Every SBSP proposal that frames the value proposition as "clean electricity delivered to ground via microwave or laser beam to terrestrial rectennas" has failed economically — because that framing forces the cost comparison against terrestrial renewables at $0.02-0.05/kWh, and the 8-stage ground-delivery conversion chain is so lossy that the comparison fails at any plausible launch cost.

NASA's January 2024 Office of Technology, Policy, and Strategy assessment[^1] is the cleanest example. The OTPS team assumed $1,000/kg launch (with a 15% block-buy discount) and reported that, with those inputs, the levelized cost of electricity for Representative Design 1 (a Mankins SPS-ALPHA-style modular swarm) is $0.61/kWh. For Representative Design 2 (Tethered-SPS, the Japanese-research-anchored design) the LCOE is $1.59/kWh.[^1] Terrestrial renewables sit at $0.02-0.05/kWh. The OTPS conclusion: SBSP is 12-31x (RD1) or 32-80x (RD2) more expensive than 2050 terrestrial projections.[^1] NASA's combined-sensitivity case — cutting launch in half, doubling hardware lifetime, applying learning-curve improvements across modules/servicers/ADR vehicles, and a 15% solar-cell-efficiency improvement — reduces LCOE to $0.03/kWh for RD1 and $0.08/kWh for RD2. This is, finally, competitive with the terrestrial range. But the combined-sensitivity case is *conditional on every input bending simultaneously*. No single change does it.

The reason the comparison is so brutal: NASA's 8-stage SBSP-to-ground conversion chain yields ~13.0% end-to-end efficiency — *solar cell 35% × DC-to-DC 90% × DC-to-RF 70% × antenna emission 90% × atmospheric transmission 98% × beam collection 95% × rectenna reception 78% × ground DC-to-DC 90%*.[^1] Of those eight stages, the last four (DC-to-RF, antenna emission, atmospheric, rectenna, ground DC-DC) exist *only because power is being beamed to ground*. They collectively destroy more than half of the orbital power before it reaches a terrestrial customer. The model's `sbsp_to_ground_lcoe()` function (see Appendix A) reproduces NASA's RD1 LCOE: at $200/kg projected, $0.0168/kWh; at $3,600/kg current, $0.3019/kWh.

**The helio chain's response: reject the SBSP-to-ground framing entirely.** All electricity generated in orbit is consumed in orbit — by orbital compute, in-space manufacturing, laser-based beam propulsion to Mars cargo, and (eventually) high-energy-physics infrastructure. The relevant conversion chain is **only the first two stages of NASA's 8-stage chain plus a few realistic orbital loss-stages**: PV cell (~35%) × DC-DC (~90%) × MPPT (~97%) × point-of-load distribution (~96%) × BOL/EOL averaging (~80%) × temperature derating (~94%) ≈ **~24% orbital-only end-to-end** (v0.11 efficiency-tightening per engineering audit; was 31.5% in v0.10). Still ~1.85× the SBSP-to-ground number; the 4 ground-delivery stages (DC-RF, antenna, atmospheric, rectenna, ground DC-DC) are the dominant losses, not the orbital-only chain. The relevant cost comparison is NOT helio chain $/kWh vs terrestrial renewables; it is helio chain $/kWh-delivered-to-orbital-consumer vs Suncatcher-class $/kWh-from-self-launched-integrated-PV ($0.0925/kWh at $200/kg launch per Suncatcher §2.4[^3]). The model's `orbital_power_cost()` function (see Appendix A) returns directly: at projected $200/kg launch, **$0.0091/kWh** — roughly 10× below the Suncatcher self-launched-PV baseline, and against a comparable orbital alternative rather than against a fundamentally different terrestrial product.

*What about reflected sunlight to ground?* Photons are not generated power. Reflector-services-to-ground wedges (premium illumination per mission + thermal warming + PV augmentation + greenhouse photons) deliver sunlight that has been redirected via thin-film mirror, not electricity generated by orbital PV. These wedges share the helio chain's reflector infrastructure but bypass the lossy ground-delivery efficiency chain entirely — they have their own physics constraints (mirror divergence, atmospheric transmission of sunlight, target-illumination geometry) but not the SBSP-to-ground efficiency trap. See section 3.1 for the wedge-by-wedge breakdown.

**This is the article's central architectural decision.** §1.2-§1.5 below cover four supporting traps — launch-cost optimism, workload-class fit, stalled-progress sensitivity, regulatory critical-path — but the dominant trap is the product premise itself. Address the dominant trap (reject SBSP-to-ground; consume orbital electricity in orbit) and the supporting traps become tractable. Fall into the dominant trap and the supporting traps compound until the economics are unsalvageable, as NASA OTPS demonstrates.

### 1.2 Supporting trap: launch-cost premise too optimistic

The fatal pattern is to assume a launch cost that is one or two decades ahead of what is actually demonstrated, and then build the whole LCOE calculation against that assumed number. When the assumed cost is challenged, the entire economics collapses because launch is the single dominant cost driver.

NASA's January 2024 Office of Technology, Policy, and Strategy assessment[^1] is the cleanest example of the trap done *honestly*. The OTPS team explicitly assumed $1,000/kg launch (with a 15% block-buy discount) and reported that, with those inputs, the levelized cost of electricity for Representative Design 1 (a Mankins SPS-ALPHA-style modular swarm) is $0.61/kWh. For Representative Design 2 (Tethered-SPS, the Japanese-research-anchored design) the LCOE is $1.59/kWh.[^1] Terrestrial renewables sit at $0.02-0.05/kWh. The OTPS conclusion: SBSP is 12-31x (RD1) or 32-80x (RD2) more expensive than 2050 terrestrial projections.[^1]

What NASA did *right* is that they showed exactly which input dominated. Launch alone is 71% of RD1 lifecycle cost and 77% of RD2 lifecycle cost.[^1] Combined with manufacturing (the second cost driver at 22% and 18% respectively),[^1] launch-plus-manufacturing accounts for over 93% of everything. Improve launch cost or manufacturing cost in isolation, and the LCOE moves; improve neither, and it doesn't.

What John Mankins, who designed RD1, pointed out in response[^2] is that NASA's $1,000/kg launch assumption was *itself* pessimistic given the documented Starship trajectory. SpaceX's published learning-curve analysis (Google's Project Suncatcher white paper,[^3] which is also building against this premise) suggests launch costs may reach $200/kg by the mid-2030s — but only if Starship achieves a 20% historical learning rate sustained through 1,800 additional launches and 370,000 tons of additional cumulative mass launched.[^3]

The helio chain's response: *frame launch cost as the single most-sensitive parameter, model it explicitly across the full $50-$3,600/kg range, and include a stalled-progress sensitivity case that pins it at current cost.* The parametric model output (v0.11): at $3,600/kg today, orbital-power cost is $0.1635/kWh. At $200/kg projected, it falls to $0.0091/kWh (orbital-only chain at ~24% efficiency per the v0.11 audit-tightening). At the in-between scenarios, cost drops monotonically with launch cost. The article's economics live or die on whether launch cost actually bends.

### 1.3 Supporting trap: workload-class fit not specified

This trap shows up specifically in the orbital-compute variant of helio-chain proposals (Google's Suncatcher, Starcloud, China's Three-Body Computing Constellation, SpaceX's January 2026 FCC filing for one million data-center satellites, and others). The trap: claim that orbital compute will be cheaper than terrestrial compute, without specifying which workload classes. In reality, different compute workloads have radically different bandwidth, latency, and synchronization requirements, and orbital compute fails for the bandwidth-heaviest workloads (notably AI training batch) while excelling for the bandwidth-lightest (Bitcoin proof-of-work, AI inference batch).

The parametric model exposes this with workload-class-specific multipliers. Bitcoin PoW: 1.0× revenue multiplier (no bandwidth penalty). AI inference: 0.85× (low input/output bandwidth per call). AI training batch: 0.10× (massive checkpoint synchronization currently prohibitive across inter-satellite optical links). Scientific simulation: 0.40× (output-bound). Rendering: 0.60× (input small, output medium, latency-flexible).

The result, validated against external sources: Bitcoin PoW closes net-positive at approximately $623/kg launch cost given Starcloud-class power density (1 kW per 12 kg satellite mass, anchored to the Starcloud-1 H100 demonstrator[^4]). AI inference closes at $529/kg. AI training batch requires launch costs near $62/kg before it closes — and would also require ~100x improvements in inter-satellite optical bandwidth that the Suncatcher paper explicitly identifies as a current limitation.[^3] Forethought's analysis[^5] independently reaches the same conclusion: "inter-satellite bandwidth limitations likely confine ODCs to inference workloads, at least early on."

![Workload break-even costs: 5 workload classes with the Suncatcher $200/kg + Starcloud $500/kg + Falcon 9 $3,600/kg reference lines drawn. Bitcoin PoW closes first (~$623/kg) as a no-bandwidth workload; AI training batch closes last (~$62/kg) because inter-satellite optical bandwidth is currently prohibitive.](v2_charts/chart_2_workload_breakeven.png)

This is good news for the helio-chain proposal IF the early-stage workload mix is restricted to bandwidth-light compute. It is bad news if the marketing positioning attempts to claim universal orbital-compute superiority. The article does not make that claim.

### 1.4 Supporting trap: stalled-progress sensitivity ignored

Most orbital-power articles model the optimistic case and stop. They do not ask: what if launch cost plateaus higher than projected? What if Moore's-Law-via-specialization stalls? What if AI capability per watt diminishing-returns is closer than current estimates? What if in-space manufacturing never matures past Varda's current pilot scale?

This is a critical analytical hole because the entire helio-chain thesis rests on four simultaneously-bending curves over a 20-30 year horizon. The historical base rate for any single technology curve continuing to improve at a current rate for 20-30 years is significantly less than 100%. The joint probability of *all four* curves continuing is meaningfully less than any individual probability.

The model handles this with explicit stalled-progress scenarios. For each of the four curves, the scenario pins that one curve to its current (worst) value while the other three continue per assumption. The results:

- **Launch cost stalls at $3,600/kg:** orbital-power cost rises from $0.0091/kWh (projected) to $0.1635/kWh (current) — both direct `orbital_power_cost()` outputs at v0.11 efficiency-tightening (was $0.0069/$0.1246 at v0.10's 0.315 efficiency). Orbital-compute wedge fails; Phase 1 reflector-services wedges (premium illumination only post-2026-05-26 thermal-warming retraction) continue to operate. *This is the most sensitive curve for the orbital-power-consuming wedges.*
- **Robotics + in-space manufacturing stalls (mfg cost stays at 2× launch instead of dropping to mature 0.10× launch):** orbital-power cost rises proportionally with mfg multiplier. Material but recoverable; the four-wedge structure absorbs the partial impact. *Materially impactful but not fatal.*
- **Compute cost stalls (orbital pricing pinned to terrestrial-DC floor of $570/kW/yr):** orbital-power cost unaffected (this curve affects compute-wedge REVENUE not orbital-power-COST). The compute wedge specifically fails; other wedges (reflector services to ground, beam propulsion, orbital-power-as-a-service to other orbital customers) continue. *Wedge-specific, not systemic.*
- **AI capability per watt stalls (perf/watt frozen at 2026 levels):** orbital-power cost unaffected for the same reason. AI training batch stays bandwidth-prohibitive forever, but the other 4 workload classes are unaffected. *Wedge-specific, not systemic.*

The honest read: the helio chain has ONE single point of failure, and it is launch cost. The other three curves are important but recoverable. This is also good news in the sense that the entire infrastructure-bet reduces to a bet on Starship maturation. If Starship works, the helio chain is plausible. If Starship doesn't, none of it is.

### 1.5 Supporting trap: regulatory critical-path under-modeled

The orbital-power literature is dominated by engineering and economics. Regulatory exposure is consistently treated as a footnote — something to be solved after the technology works. This is backwards in several ways.

ITAR export controls already apply to several Phase 1 and Phase 2 technologies: thin-film deployable reflectors, large-aperture phased arrays, high-rate inter-satellite optical links, and especially radiation-hardened compute. ITU spectrum allocation is non-trivial for any GW-class microwave power-beaming system. OST Article IV does not explicitly ban beam-power systems but the licensing scrutiny is severe for any system that *could* be repurposed as a directed-energy weapon, which a controllable kilometer-scale orbital reflector or microwave-beaming array obviously could. FAA airspace coordination becomes meaningful when ground-rectenna sites enter active aviation corridors. Environmental review applies to upper-atmosphere effects of Starship launch cadence at the scale Suncatcher's $200/kg projection requires (180 Starship launches per year sustained for a decade).

The helio chain's response: section 4 of this article documents per-phase regulatory exposure as a structured matrix. Phase 1 reflector swarm is the lowest-exposure phase (export controls on reflector tech; astronomy-interference debate parallels the Starlink fight). Phase 4 high-energy-physics infrastructure is the highest-exposure phase (antimatter export controls, particle-physics dual-use). The regulatory regime evolution is the explicit Open Question 7 the article doesn't pretend to answer — assumes status-quo for the 20-year horizon, with a flag that liberalization or tightening would shift the build timeline.

## 2. The parametric economics model

*What this section covers: how the model encodes tier discipline (§2.1), what the four wedge categories are and why they are split this way (§2.2), what the model's four output classes are (§2.3), the keystone result that the rest of the article rests on (§2.4), and what the model explicitly does NOT do (§2.5). Skip ahead to §3 if you want phase-by-phase commentary; come back to §2 if you want to argue with the numbers.*

The model is at `economics_model/helio_chain_economics.py`. It is single-file Python (~700 lines), runs end-to-end with `python helio_chain_economics.py`, and produces a Promotion-Gate tier rollup, sample outputs, phase-trigger conditions, stalled-progress sensitivity scenarios across all 4 curves, sample wedge-revenue calculations across 4 wedge categories, and Stefan-Boltzmann radiator-mass scaling across temperatures.

### 2.1 Promotion-Gate tier discipline

Every numeric input is a `Parameter` dataclass with a tier flag from `T1` (internal direction-setting only) through `T5` (rules / code usable, empirically reproduced). The current model state (2026-05-24):

- **6 inputs at T4** (public-claim usable, primary-source verified): launch_cost ($3,600/kg current from Suncatcher §2.4, projection to $200/kg by mid-2030s); conversion_efficiency (~13.0% end-to-end product of NASA OTPS 8-stage chain); orbital_compute_revenue ($570-3,000/kW/yr terrestrial DC range from Suncatcher §2.4); ai_capability_per_watt_curve_factor (1.34× annual from arXiv 2504.16026); rectenna_footprint (14.1-66 km²/GW from NASA OTPS RD1/RD2); in_space_manufacturing_cost_multiplier (0.31× launch cost RD1 baseline from NASA OTPS).
- **5 inputs at T3** (sales-deck usable with caveat): areal_density (Reflect Orbital 0.163 kg/m² satellite + Kapton membrane floor + ROSA collector ceiling); radiator_temperature (300 K passive-thermal default); radiator_areal_density (3 kg/m² midpoint from spacecraft thermal literature); power_density_kw_per_kg (Starcloud-1 anchor 0.083 kW/kg); direct_power_as_a_service_revenue ($0.50/kWh base, range $0.10-$4.09 from NREL South Pole + Hawaii + DoD FBCF).
- **6 inputs at PLACEHOLDER** (no committed default; appear as wide ranges only): station_keeping_dv (SBSP-specific solar-pressure-perturbed values not surfaced); beam_propulsion_revenue_per_kg_payload (Phase 3 speculative); pv_augmentation_revenue (Reflect Orbital competitive); premium_illumination_revenue_per_mission (defense/SAR/event range; falsification-tested 2026-05-25[^17]); greenhouse_photon_revenue (commercial grow-light tariffs); thermal_warming_revenue (added v0.7; falsification-tested 2026-05-26[^18] across 4 customer classes).
- **1 input DEPRECATED at T3 historical** (preserved for §1.2 trap-framing reference): direct_power_as_a_service_revenue (microwave/laser to terrestrial rectenna; wedge removed from helio chain scope per v0.7 clarification).

The point of the tier discipline is to prevent unpromoted research from becoming load-bearing economics. The article cites only T3 and above when making numerical claims; ranges are reported when the discipline forbids a point estimate.

**Sidebar — demand-side trend:** the per-watt efficiency curve (1.34× annual) is being outpaced by the *total power consumption* curve for frontier AI systems (~2× annual growth in total power, per the same Epoch AI dataset[^7]). xAI's Colossus, the leading AI supercomputer as of March 2025, required ~300 MW — equivalent to 250,000 households.[^7] The projected leading 2030 system requires ~2 million chips.[^7] This means total compute power demand is rising faster than terrestrial efficiency gains can offset, which strengthens the demand-side case for orbital compute: if terrestrial power infrastructure cannot scale 2× per year indefinitely, orbital becomes the marginal supply.

### 2.2 Four-wedge revenue structure

The model splits potential revenue paths into four wedge categories rather than the conventional single-wedge framing. The split was forced by adversarial review (the airport runway de-icing and Arctic-town-warming ideas, which fail on physics, transit time, polar-night sun availability, and Reflect Orbital's competitive occupation) and the resulting 8-sub-problem decomposition.

- **Orbital compute** (Suncatcher / Starcloud / Three-Body Constellation territory): 5 workload classes with explicit bandwidth-penalty multipliers. Break-even launch cost per workload validated independently against Starcloud's published $500/kg target and Suncatcher's projected $200/kg threshold.
- **Beam propulsion** (Phase 3 wedge): revenue per kg payload accelerated to Mars trajectory. Currently PLACEHOLDER because there is no commercial precedent for beam-propulsion service pricing.
- **Reflector services trio**: PV augmentation (Reflect Orbital is the established competitor); premium illumination per mission (defense ISR, SAR corridor, disaster-zone lighting, event spectacle, construction night work, maritime SAR — 6 customer classes with WTP multipliers); greenhouse photon augmentation (narrow biology wedge for high-latitude commercial greenhouses).
- **Direct power-as-a-service** (microwave or laser beam to terrestrial rectenna): 6 customer classes — defense forward base (4× base rate), disaster recovery (3×), Antarctic research (2.5×), remote mining (1.5×), island grid (1.2×), maritime vessel (1×). T3-anchored against NREL's $4.09/kWh South Pole diesel LCOE, Hawaii's $0.4393/kWh residential rate, and DoD fully-burdened-cost-of-fuel up to $400/gallon at helicopter-only resupply.

The four-wedge structure matters because the kill case (Question 4 of the framing decisions) is "widen the workload search beyond compute" — and the model already supports it. If orbital compute does not close at any plausible launch cost, the three other wedge categories provide independent revenue paths.

### 2.3 What the model outputs

Four output classes:

- **Orbital-power cost** — Cost per kWh of orbital electricity delivered to orbital consumers (compute / manufacturing / lasers / beam-prop). Uses the orbital-only conversion chain (~24%, v0.11 audit; PV cell × DC-DC plus realistic orbital loss stages). NOT to be confused with the ~13%-efficiency SBSP-to-ground LCOE — that chain is what the article uses as the §1.2 trap framing, not the helio chain's actual product. Inputs: launch cost, system mass, lifetime, capacity factor, orbital-only conversion efficiency, manufacturing cost multiplier.
- **Compute $/kWh** — orbital compute cost per kWh at a given spacecraft mass, lifetime, and power density. Inputs: launch cost, spacecraft mass, operational lifetime, compute power.
- **Workload break-even launch cost** — the launch cost ($/kg) at which a given workload class transitions from net-loss to net-positive. Inputs: workload class, spacecraft mass, lifetime, revenue target. Output: the launch cost threshold.
- **Phase trigger conditions** — for each of the 4 phases, the launch cost threshold + tech-readiness signal + market signal that activates the phase.

Plus two harnesses:

- **1D sensitivity sweep** — vary one parameter across its placeholder range, plot the output. Useful for the tornado-diagram analysis that surfaces which input dominates which output.
- **Stalled-progress scenario** — for each of the 4 assumed curves, evaluate the orbital-power cost (or any output) under the hypothesis that the curve plateaus while the others continue per assumption.

For reference: the Suncatcher-class self-launched-PV baseline at $200/kg is $810/kW/yr ≈ $0.0925/kWh at 100% utilization — the helio chain's orbital-power cost is roughly 10× below this baseline because shared orbital-PV infrastructure amortizes across multiple consumers (compute / manufacturing / lasers / beam-prop) rather than being dedicated per-satellite.

Run the same model with the LAUNCH_COST stall (launch pins at current $3,600/kg, mature in-space manufacturing still applies): **$0.1635/kWh** — comparable to current Starlink-class launched-power-price economics ($14,700/kW/yr ≈ $1.68/kWh per Suncatcher §2.4, normalized to a per-kWh basis at full utilization). ### 2.5 What the model explicitly does NOT do

The model is not perfect. Known limitations include:

- The model's `conversion_efficiency` parameter has historically been set at ~13.0% (NASA OTPS 8-stage SBSP-to-ground chain; arithmetic-corrected 2026-06-07 from a mis-stated 14.6% — see RETRACTIONS C1). With the v0.7 scope clarification (no power beamed to ground), the operationally-relevant default is ~24% (PV cell × DC-DC plus realistic orbital loss stages; v0.11 audit, tightened from the v0.10 2-stage 31.5%). The parameter exposes both values: ~13.0% for the §1.2 trap framing comparison; ~24% for the helio chain's own product. Range and stalled-progress hooks unchanged.
- Workload-bandwidth requirements are encoded as fixed multipliers, not derived from inter-satellite optical link physics. A future v0.8 should tie the multipliers to actual bandwidth math.
- The `areal_density` parameter is overloaded — it covers both thin-film reflectors (0.011-0.020 kg/m² range) AND solar PV collectors (0.65 kg/m² ROSA-class). A future version should split into `reflector_areal_density` and `collector_areal_density` for cleaner phase-1-vs-phase-2 economics.
- The thermal warming wedge (added v0.7) was falsification-tested 2026-05-26 and RETRACTED at single-satellite scale: the initial pass used a constellation-scale per-overpass energy anchor (~35 MWh) that a single 100 kg / 614 m² satellite cannot deliver (actual ~0.15 MWh — a 230× overstatement); the physics-corrected pass fails 11/11 customer-class scenarios (see §5 and RETRACTIONS R2). Premium illumination per mission was falsification-tested 2026-05-25 (scenario-close across all 5 scenarios at $3,600/kg) — but revenue-per-mission and cadence remain placeholders, so it is commercially unvalidated.
- No regulatory cost model. Regulatory exposure is documented in section 4 below but does not propagate into the parametric model. The v0.7 reframe materially LIGHTENS the regulatory exposure (no microwave/laser power-to-ground means no GW-class beam-weapon concerns; reflector-sunlight wedges face a different and lighter regime).
- The direct_power_as_a_service wedge (microwave/laser to ground rectenna) is preserved in the model code as a deprecated reference but is no longer part of the helio chain's claimed product. It exists in the codebase to support reproducing the §1.2 trap framing.
- No financing or learning-curve depth beyond the inputs NASA OTPS uses (75% modules, 85% servicers, 90% ADR vehicles). Real economics depend on discount rate, schedule risk, and demonstrated learning at scale.

The discipline is to document limitations explicitly and avoid making claims the model cannot support.

## 3. The four phases gated on tech-readiness

*What this section covers: the four-phase build plan with explicit tech-readiness and market-signal gates rather than calendar dates. Phase 1 (§3.1) starts now and is launch-cost-tolerant (per the 2026-05-25 falsification[^17]). Phase 2 (§3.2) requires launch ≤$500/kg and runs a hybrid GPU/TPU fleet in two parallel revenue modes (compute-as-a-service + Bitcoin mining fallback). Phase 3 (§3.3) requires launch ≤$200/kg + demonstrated 1+ MW in-space laser beaming for Mars cargo. Phase 4 (§3.4) is in-orbit high-energy-physics infrastructure, not Earth-receiving.*

The helio chain's build sequence is four phases, each gated on tech-readiness signals rather than calendar dates. The calendar matters less than the curve-bending; if launch cost drops faster than projected, phase triggers fire earlier, and vice versa.

### 3.1 Phase 1 — Reflector swarm (any launch cost)

**Tech-readiness signal:** Large deployable structures at TRL ≥7 (ROSA-class) demonstrated on multiple flights. **STATUS: CURRENTLY MET.** ROSA's 2017 ISS spaceflight experiment validated the deployable architecture at 0.65 kg/m² and 400 W/kg specific power.[^12] Redwire's July 2025 deployment of 60 kW ROSA wings for the Lunar Gateway Power and Propulsion Element further validates the manufacturing supply chain.[^12]

**Market signal:** First polar-PV-winter-augmentation pilot customer signed. **STATUS: REFLECT ORBITAL OCCUPIES THIS WEDGE.** Reflect Orbital raised $20M Series A (Lux Capital + Sequoia + Starship Ventures), secured a $1.25M US Air Force contract, and reports 260,000 customer applications from 157 countries for "sunlight as a service." Their target: 50,000 mirrors in orbit by 2030, $5,000/hour per mirror pricing plus revenue-sharing with solar farm operators.[^10]

The helio chain's Phase 1 positioning is **not** to compete head-on with Reflect Orbital in the PV-augmentation wedge but to concentrate on the four reflector sub-wedges Reflect Orbital has explicitly de-prioritized:

1. **Premium illumination per mission** — short-duration (5-30 min) reflected-sunlight events directed at named ground targets. Customer classes: defense ISR / SAR corridor / disaster-zone lighting / commercial events / construction night work / maritime SAR. Pricing per mission, not per kWh.
2. **Thermal warming service** (new sub-wedge in v0.7) — sustained reflected sunlight directed at ground targets for thermal heating purposes. Initial customer classes: airport runway warming in clear winter conditions (preventing ice formation); small mining-town diesel-heating displacement; Arctic-base winter heating. Forward-looking expansion: cold-city winter "temperature-control-pay-per-service" (Toronto / Helsinki / Anchorage class — gated on social-license maturation since visible orbital infrastructure over populated cities raises a separate trust question). Pricing per heating-degree-day delivered or per hour of sustained reflection, depending on customer class.
3. **Greenhouse photon augmentation** — reflected sunlight to high-latitude commercial greenhouses, competing with HPS / LED grow-light electricity. Narrow biology wedge.
4. **PV augmentation** — reflected sunlight onto terrestrial PV farms during dawn / dusk / cloud-cover windows. Reflect Orbital's primary wedge; helio chain de-prioritizes head-on entry here.

The choice of Phase 1 entry wedge is a portfolio decision the helio chain can re-balance as customer demand signals arrive.

**Architectural note.** All four Phase 1 sub-wedges share the same **reflector physics** — a thin-film aluminized-mylar reflector at low areal density (0.011-0.020 kg/m² for reflector membrane; 0.163 kg/m² for full satellite per Reflect Orbital anchor) in LEO sun-sync orbit, redirecting natural sunlight to ground targets. **No electricity is generated, converted, or beamed** by any of these wedges — photons reflect off the mirror and travel to the target via atmospheric transmission. This is fundamentally different from SBSP-to-ground power-beaming (which the v0.6 article and prior literature called "direct power-as-a-service"). The helio chain explicitly DOES NOT pursue power-beaming-to-ground — see section 1.2 for why that's the trap, not the product.

### 3.2.1 Heat rejection — the actual hard problem

The objection a thermal engineer raises first: vacuum is the best insulator we know of. In orbit there is no air to blow across a heat sink and no water to pump to a cooling tower. The *only* channel for shedding waste heat is radiating it away as infrared into the ~3 K sky. For an orbital data center, heat rejection — not power supply — is the genuinely hard part, and it is the term that an engineering audit forced into the integrated-mass model (see §2.5, CRIT-2).

The physics sets the price through the Stefan–Boltzmann law: radiated power per unit area scales as the *fourth power* of radiator temperature, `P/A = εσT⁴`. The model's `radiator_mass_per_mw_thermal()` function (ε = 0.85, two-sided panels, `radiator_areal_density` 3 kg/m² T3 anchor) makes the consequence concrete:

| Radiator temp | Radiating area (two-sided) | Radiator mass |
|---|---|---|
| 250 K | 2,656 m²/MW | 7,968 kg/MW |
| 300 K (passive default) | 1,281 m²/MW | 3,842 kg/MW |
| 400 K | 405 m²/MW | 1,216 kg/MW |
| 500 K | 166 m²/MW | 498 kg/MW |
| 800 K (reactor-side loop) | 25 m²/MW | 76 kg/MW |

The T⁴ scaling is the whole story. Rejecting a gigawatt of waste heat at a passive 300 K needs ~3,840 tonnes of radiator — a sizable fraction of the ~6,510 t solar array feeding 1 GW electrical (§2.5). That is not a term you can wish away, which is exactly why the integrated Phase 2 compute-payload mass (~22,400 t/GW: PV + compute hardware + radiator) is ~3.4× the PV-only figure and narrows the dominance gap against Suncatcher's integrated baseline.

But the same T⁴ law is the lever, with an important honest caveat. Running the heat-rejection loop hotter collapses radiator mass — **~50× lighter at 800 K than at 300 K**. The catch: a radiator can only run as hot as the heat source feeding it. Compute silicon caps at ~350–400 K junction temperatures, so a compute-coupled radiator lives in the ~1,200–2,000 kg/MW band, not the 76 kg/MW best case. The dramatic high-temperature regime belongs to genuinely hot sources — fusion blankets, reactor-side loops — which is precisely why **Phase 4's high-temperature physics infrastructure is where orbit's cold sky pays off most**: the hotter the source, the more decisively cheaper it is to cool in vacuum than under the cooling-tower constraints that bound every terrestrial design.

The architectures to get there already exist in concept: pumped high-temperature single-phase or two-phase loops moving heat to large deployable panels; two-sided panels radiating into the dark on both faces; and liquid-droplet radiators that spray hot droplets directly into space and recapture them downstream, shedding the dead structural mass of solid panels entirely (a NASA-studied concept since the 1980s). None is free, none is exotic-beyond-engineering.

The honest summary: heat rejection is a real, T⁴-steep mass overhead — already carried in the cost model, bounded, and worst exactly where ambitions are coolest (commodity compute) and lightest exactly where they are hottest (Phase 4 physics). The cold sky is not free cooling. But it *is* cooling, and it gets cheaper as the source gets hotter.

### 4.1 Historical analogs — what the regulatory trajectory of analogous infrastructures tells us

Three historical analogs are worth examining for whether they predict liberalization or tightening of the orbital-power regulatory regime.

**Commercial satellite communications (1960s-1990s):** the original Comsat Act of 1962 created a regulated monopoly structure that constrained commercial deployment for decades. Liberalization came in stages — the Open Skies policy of the 1990s, the eventual breakup of Intelsat into commercial entities, and culminating in the 2018 Open Market Reentry Act and related reforms that finally treated commsats as commercial infrastructure rather than diplomatic strategic assets.[^14] The trajectory was liberalization, but slow — ~30 years from "obviously commercial" to "regulated like other commercial industries." The helio chain's orbital-power infrastructure is at the same kind of "obviously commercial" stage that commsats were at in the early 1980s. If the analog holds, full commercial-regulatory normalization is a multi-decade process.

**Commercial nuclear power (1950s-present):** the trajectory here is *less* hopeful for the helio chain. Initially treated as a national-security strategic asset, commercial nuclear was always heavily-regulated. The NRC's licensing process averages 10-15 years per new reactor.[^14] Compounding regulatory caution after Three Mile Island (1979), Chernobyl (1986), and Fukushima (2011) means even mature commercial nuclear faces high regulatory friction. The lesson: if the helio chain's beam-power infrastructure is interpreted as analogous to nuclear (high-energy, high-consequence-if-misused, low public trust), expect 10-20 year licensing timelines per new system. The mitigation: avoid the framing analogy. Position helio-chain reflectors and microwave beaming as fundamentally different from nuclear in failure modes (no radioactive contamination; no waste stream; well-bounded worst-case beam-loss scenarios).

**Commercial space launch (Falcon 9 era, 2008-present):** the trajectory here is *encouraging* for the helio chain. Commercial launch went from heavily-regulated single-provider (Atlas/Delta with extensive military oversight) to commercially-dominant (SpaceX + competitors) over ~15 years. FAA-AST licensing matured quickly when there was commercial demand.[^14] The lesson: regulatory frameworks adapt when commercial pressure is clear and when industry self-organizes to advocate. The helio chain benefits from being a beneficiary of this trajectory (Starship is the upstream dependency) but is also subject to similar dynamics for its own regulatory regime.

The honest read across the three analogs: regulatory normalization is *possible* but not *automatic*. It requires (a) clear commercial demand signals, (b) industry self-organization to advocate, (c) absence of high-consequence early-failure events that compound public caution, and (d) decades of accumulated regulatory precedent. The helio chain is exposed in (c) specifically — a high-visibility early failure (e.g., a beam-pointing mishap causing a wildfire or an aviation incident) could set the regulatory regime back 20+ years. Risk mitigation: deploy with extreme safety margins on early systems; over-engineer the beam-control and abort systems; participate in transparent incident reporting.

### 4.2 The OST Article IV question in detail

Article IV of the Outer Space Treaty (1967)[^15] is the single regulatory provision most relevant to the helio chain's existential viability. The operative first paragraph states verbatim: "States Parties to the Treaty undertake not to place in orbit around the Earth any objects carrying nuclear weapons or any other kinds of weapons of mass destruction, install such weapons on celestial bodies, or station such weapons in outer space in any other manner."[^15]

Three observations:

1. **What Article IV actually bans:** "weapons of mass destruction" (WMD), specifically nuclear, biological, chemical, and radiological. NOT dual-use civilian infrastructure. NOT conventional military systems. NOT directed-energy systems per se.

2. **What Article IV is ambiguous about:** controllable kilometer-scale orbital infrastructure that *could be repurposed* as a directed-energy weapon. A GW-class microwave-beaming system aimed at a friendly rectenna is civilian infrastructure; the same system aimed at a hostile asset is a weapon. The treaty does not address whether the *capability* itself is allowed.

3. **What the silence means in practice:** since 1967, no orbital system has been formally challenged under Article IV. The default interpretation has been generous (commercial satellites are not WMD; nuclear-powered probes like Cassini are not WMD because the nuclear material is RTG fuel, not weapons). The helio chain's beam-power infrastructure will eventually force a formal interpretation. The question is whether the precedent gets set by a friendly system (helio chain itself, or a friendly competitor) or by an adversarial test case.

The helio chain's strategic posture: advocate for a clear interpretation that distinguishes commercial beam-power infrastructure from weapon systems by reference to specific technical criteria — beam-pointing constraints, abort mechanisms, target coordination protocols, transparency in operations. The advocacy needs to happen *before* the first GW-class system flies, not after. This is a 10-year lead-time concern.

### 4.3 Where regulatory cost enters the economics

The parametric model does NOT include regulatory cost as a direct LCOE input. This is a known gap and worth quantifying. Empirical anchors from analogous industries:

- **Commercial nuclear:** licensing + compliance costs run 15-25% of total project capex.[^6] For a $10B SBSP system, this would be $1.5-2.5B in regulatory overhead.
- **Commercial space launch:** FAA-AST licensing is comparatively cheap — typically <1% of mission cost.[^6] Spectrum coordination via ITU is similar.
- **Pharmaceuticals:** FDA approval can be 20-40% of total development cost.[^6]

The helio chain's regulatory cost likely sits between commercial-space (low end) and commercial-nuclear (high end), depending on which analog the regime adopts. A reasonable planning range is 5-15% of total system capex. At the projected $200/kg launch cost with $1B SBSP system capex, this adds $50-150M to the system cost — material but not fatal. At current launch costs where total system capex is far higher, the regulatory percentage applied to a much larger base becomes more impactful.

The v0.3 model should add `regulatory_cost_fraction` as a parameter in v0.4 — currently PLACEHOLDER; would promote to T3 with the analogs above as anchors.

## 5. What fails if any curve stalls

*What this section covers: per-curve stalled-progress analysis (§5.1-§5.5), the midpoint-scenario finding that surfaces a different kind of failure (§5.6), the kill case with research-portfolio salvage (§5.7), build-sequencing implications (§5.8), and the 17 enumerated open questions the article does NOT pretend to resolve (§5.9). This is the article's most-load-bearing section for adversarial review.*

This section operationalizes the kill case (Question 4 of the framing decisions): *if compute economics never close even at $50/kg, the article's conclusion is to widen the workload search beyond compute, not to shelve the helio chain entirely.* The four-wedge structure of the model already supports this.

### 5.1 The four curves and their joint dependency

The four assumed curves are:

1. **Launch cost continues declining** — current $3,600/kg → projected $200/kg by mid-2030s (Suncatcher anchor) → $50/kg dominance threshold (Forethought).
2. **Compute cost-per-FLOP continues improving via specialization** — current 1.34× annual perf/watt (arXiv 2504.16026) → continued specialized accelerator scaling.
3. **AI capability per watt continues advancing** — same 1.34× annual baseline, with diminishing returns visible at transformer-parameter scale.
4. **Robotics and in-space manufacturing continue maturing** — current Varda + Axiom + Redwire pilot scale → mature in-space manufacturing displaces launched hardware (mfg cost 0.10× launch instead of NASA RD1 0.31× baseline).

The model's stalled-progress analysis shows the asymmetric impact. Launch cost stalling is the single dominant failure mode. Robotics stalling is material but recoverable. Compute and AI stalling affect specific wedges (orbital compute) without affecting LCOE.

### 5.2 If launch cost stalls

This is the single fatal stall for the orbital-power-consuming wedges (Phase 2+). With launch cost pinned at $3,600/kg and the other three curves continuing, `orbital_power_cost()` returns **$0.1246/kWh** — comparable to the Suncatcher-class current launched-power-price baseline of $14,700/kW/yr at current launch costs. Orbital compute break-even costs move to the right by ~5×: Bitcoin PoW now requires ~$3,000/kg launch to close (rather than $623); AI training batch never closes.

The Phase 1 reflector services to ground (premium illumination per mission; thermal warming retracted 2026-05-26 per R2) are NOT affected by the launch-cost stall in the same way — the 100 kg satellite class means launch capex is small enough that even at $3,600/kg premium illumination is scenario-close per the 2026-05-25 falsification[^17] (revenue and cadence still placeholder). Phase 1 commercial revenue continues regardless of Starship's trajectory.

The helio chain's response if launch cost stalls: **Phase 1 continues; Phase 2+ waits**. The bet on the four curves bending becomes a bet on the launch-cost curve specifically. If Starship works, the orbital-power-consuming wedges open; if Starship plateaus, the helio chain reduces to the Phase 1 reflector-services business. There is no clever architectural workaround that meaningfully reduces launch-cost sensitivity for orbital compute / manufacturing / beam-prop.

### 5.3 If robotics + in-space manufacturing stalls

In-space manufacturing matters because it determines whether spacecraft are launched as finished hardware (current model: ~$1B for a Mankins-style integrated orbital module) or assembled from launched components by in-orbit robotics (mature model: components launched cheaply, integration in orbit, total cost lower because the launched mass is smaller and the manufacturing infrastructure is reused).

The model treats this as `in_space_manufacturing_cost_multiplier` — the ratio of manufacturing cost to launch cost. NASA RD1 baseline is 0.31× (manufacturing is 22% of lifecycle / launch is 71%). The stalled scenario pins this at 2.0× (manufacturing never matures; every spacecraft launched fully assembled). Result: orbital-power cost rises proportionally with the manufacturing multiplier — roughly a 2.7× increase in capex-amortized $/kWh delivered to orbital consumers.

This is material but recoverable. The helio chain survives the robotics stall — Phase 1 reflector services to ground are essentially unaffected (small 100 kg satellites; manufacturing share is a smaller fraction of total cost), and Phase 2+ orbital-power-consuming wedges still close at favorable launch costs, just less profitably. The four-wedge structure absorbs the partial impact.

### 5.4 If compute cost stalls

Compute cost stalling affects the orbital-compute wedge's *compute-as-a-service mode* specifically — the revenue ceiling for selling compute capacity to external ODC customers is bounded by terrestrial DC pricing $570-3,000/kW/yr (Suncatcher §2.4). If terrestrial compute does not get cheaper than this floor, orbital compute can never undercut, regardless of launch cost.

**But the hybrid hardware fleet has a Bitcoin-mining fallback.** The Phase 2 hardware that runs compute-as-a-service is the *same hardware* that mines Bitcoin during idle time. Bitcoin mining revenue is bounded by Bitcoin price × emission / network hashrate, NOT by terrestrial DC pricing. Compute-cost stall has no direct effect on the mining mode — mining stays profitable for orbital miners as long as orbital-power cost is below the marginal terrestrial-miner electricity-cost-equivalent (~$0.03-0.05/kWh in 2026 conditions). **Under compute-cost stall the same hardware simply shifts from compute-as-a-service mode to Bitcoin-mining mode** — utilization of the orbital fleet stays high; revenue rate drops from compute-as-a-service rates to BTC-mining rates. The fleet does NOT go idle. This is the structural advantage of the hybrid architecture vs a dedicated compute-only fleet: the BTC fallback is a baseline revenue floor that survives the compute-cost stall.

The other wedge categories (reflector services quartet to ground, beam propulsion, orbital-power-as-a-service to other orbital customers) are unaffected because they do not route through compute economics either. The article's conclusion: this is the scenario where the kill case fires for compute-as-a-service. We lean harder on the Phase 1 reflector-services revenue (premium illumination at single-satellite spectacle pricing has wedge-specific economics that do not require the compute-as-a-service revenue ceiling), keep the Bitcoin mining mode running on whatever Phase 2 hardware is launched, keep Phase 3 beam-propulsion as a Mars-cargo-market bet, and treat compute-as-a-service as a possible-future-upside rather than the primary Phase 2 revenue path.

### 5.5 If AI capability per watt stalls

Similar wedge-specific impact. AI training batch stays bandwidth-prohibitive; AI inference and the other non-AI workloads are unaffected. The compute wedge survives in a reduced form; the broader helio chain is unaffected.

### 5.6 The midpoint scenario — also uneconomic

When I built the first version of the parametric model, I expected the single-curve stall analysis to be the headline finding. It showed nicely-bounded behavior — launch cost is by far the most-sensitive input; other curves matter less. Tidy.

Then I ran a separate analysis where, instead of stalling one curve while the others continued, I pinned ALL 16 model inputs to their midpoint values simultaneously. The result was not tidy at all.

A separate analysis (best/midpoint/worst-case scenarios across all 16 model inputs, with each input pinned to its favorable / midpoint / unfavorable bound based on the input's cost-vs-benefit role) surfaced a finding that the single-curve stall analysis above hides: **the midpoint scenario is also uneconomic**.

At the midpoint scenario, launch cost is $1,825/kg — the range midpoint of the $50-$3,600/kg distribution, *not* a statistical median (the model has no distributions or priors over launch outcomes; "midpoint" here is the arithmetic centerpoint of the bounds, not the modal outcome). This is above Starcloud's published $500/kg break-even threshold for orbital compute. The midpoint manufacturing multiplier is 1.05× (range midpoint of 0.10× mature → 2.0× stalled), well above the NASA RD1 baseline 0.31× anchor. Direct `orbital_power_cost()` output at midpoint inputs (v0.11 efficiency-tightening): **$0.1545/kWh** — well above the Suncatcher self-launched-PV baseline of $0.0925/kWh at $200/kg. Across all five orbital-compute workload classes, the midpoint case shows **uniform loss** — no workload class closes net-positive.

The orbital-compute wedge only closes in the **lower half** of the joint launch-cost distribution. Best case (launch $50/kg, mfg 0.10×, other inputs favorable): `orbital_power_cost()` returns **$0.0023/kWh** (well below Suncatcher baseline). Worst case (launch $3,600/kg, mfg 2.0× stalled): **$0.4459/kWh** (~4.8× Suncatcher baseline). All values are direct model outputs as of v0.11.

This is a uniform-across-workloads finding that is worth surfacing because it sharpens the bet considerably. The helio chain is not betting on "average" outcomes; it is betting on a *specifically optimistic* set of outcomes for launch cost. The 4-wedge model accommodates this by providing three other revenue paths that close at higher launch costs (reflector services quartet to ground + beam propulsion + orbital-power-as-a-service to other orbital customers all have wedge-specific economics that do not require the orbital-compute break-even threshold).

The honest read: **the helio chain plan needs Starship to do better than the range-midpoint forecast, OR needs the Phase 1 reflector-services revenue to carry the early years.** Both are possible. Premium illumination's launch-cost-tolerance at spectacle-tier pricing (per the 2026-05-25 falsification[^17] + 2026-05-26 v0.12 physics-deliverable audit) provides single-satellite year-one revenue at a narrower TAM than v0.11 framed; the higher-WTP customer classes and the orbital-compute upside both require either Starship maturation or constellation-scale buildout.

12. **Thermal warming wedge — physics correction + constellation-scale economics (RETRACTED 2026-05-26 v0.11).** v0.7 added thermal warming as a Phase 1 sub-wedge. An initial 2026-05-26 falsification appeared to validate the wedge across 11 scenarios, but used a Solspace per-pass anchor (~35 MWh thermal per 17-min overpass) that describes a constellation-coordinated array of ~62,500 m² mirrors, NOT a single 100 kg / 614 m² Reflect-Orbital-class satellite. A thermodynamics engineering audit caught this 230× dimensional overstatement. The physics-corrected falsification (0.15 MWh/overpass for a 100 kg satellite) **FAILS at all tested customer classes + launch costs** at single-satellite scale. Constellation-scale economics could in principle work but cost scales linearly with satellite count; the wedge is recast as a Phase 2-3 research-portfolio item, NOT a Phase 1 launch-cost-tolerant entry point. Remaining open questions: (a) constellation-scale unit-economics not yet modeled; (b) whether thermal warming can ever compete on $/kWh-thermal given heating-fuel costs ($0.10-$0.50/kWh-thermal from displaced natural gas / diesel); (c) social-license constraints for visible orbital infrastructure overhead populated areas; (d) primary-source WTP anchors not yet promoted to T3.
13. **Phase 1 wedge unit-economics for the PLACEHOLDER sub-wedges (PARTIALLY RESOLVED 2026-05-26 v0.12).** Premium illumination per mission was falsification-tested 2026-05-25[^17] (5/5 CLOSE at defense-ISR pricing tier) and then physics-deliverable-audited 2026-05-26 (v0.12 reframe — single-satellite delivers ~1 lux, which supports only spectacle-tier pricing $1K-$50K per event; defense-grade tiers require constellation OR 10-tonne satellite). Spectacle-tier re-run: 5/6 scenarios CLOSE at $3,600/kg. Thermal warming was physics-corrected-falsified 2026-05-26[^18] — single-satellite scale FAILS at all tested launch costs; constellation-scale economics deferred to research-portfolio. Greenhouse photon augmentation and PV augmentation have NOT been falsification-tested. PV augmentation is competitively occupied by Reflect Orbital; greenhouse photons is a narrow biology wedge. **Phase 1 launch-cost-tolerance currently rests on ONE validated sub-wedge at NARROWER scope (single-satellite spectacle-tier premium illumination).** The claim becomes fully load-bearing only when the underlying revenue parameter is promoted to T3 (Reflect Orbital corporate revenue disclosure at spectacle pricing tier).
14. **Premium illumination cadence stress + physics-deliverable.** The 2026-05-25 falsification[^17] modeled 20-150 missions/yr on a single satellite. A single LEO sun-sync satellite has a theoretical maximum of ~5 illumination opportunities per day (depending on orbit altitude + ground-target latitude + revisit cadence), or ~1,800/yr. The aggressive 150 missions/yr case is 8% of theoretical max; the spectacle-tier 200 missions/yr (v0.12) is 11% of theoretical max. Real-world cadence is constrained by customer demand pacing — Reflect Orbital's 260,000 customer applications validate that aggregate demand exists but do not validate that individual customer classes will commit at the cadences modeled. v0.12 physics-deliverable audit further constrained customer mix: defense ISR / SAR / disaster / construction customers require deliverables a single 100 kg satellite cannot provide (1 lux delivered vs 50-200+ lux required); only event-spectacle / atmospheric-effect customers are physically defensible at single-satellite scale. Constellation coordination (Reflect Orbital playbook: 50,000 mirrors targeted by 2030) is the path to broader customer classes.
15. **Direct power-as-a-service (microwave/laser to ground rectenna) — definitively REMOVED v0.7.** This wedge appeared in v0.2-v0.6 of the article and was modeled as a Phase 1 sub-wedge. The 2026-05-25 falsification[^16] showed the wedge does not close at current $3,600/kg launch even at WTP-ceiling pricing for Antarctic / island-grid / disaster / mining customers; closes only for defense FB at WTP ceiling. Then v0.7 scope clarification confirmed the architecture (SBSP-to-ground via microwave/laser beam) is explicitly NOT part of the helio chain — power generated in orbit stays in orbit; reflected sunlight to ground is fine but generated electricity is not beamed down. The wedge is preserved in the model code as a deprecated reference and as a numerical example for the §1.2 trap framing. This is no longer an open question; it is a closed scope boundary.
16. **Bitcoin mining economics: halving / price-volatility / network-hashrate risk + hybrid-hardware ASIC-vs-GPU efficiency.** The Phase 2 Bitcoin mining fallback mode (added v0.7+) assumes the marginal terrestrial-miner electricity-cost-equivalent stays in the $0.03-$0.05/kWh band through the helio chain's Phase 2 deployment window. This floor depends on (a) Bitcoin price trajectory — currently $60-100K/BTC range, but historically swings ±50% within 2-year windows; (b) network difficulty growth — historically 30-50% annually; (c) halving cycle position — the next halving (May 2028) cuts block reward from 3.125 BTC to 1.5625 BTC, mechanically halving miner revenue per joule unless BTC price doubles to compensate. If BTC price falls AND difficulty rises AND a halving fires simultaneously, the terrestrial-miner floor could compress to ~$0.01-$0.02/kWh, pushing the orbital break-even launch cost upward correspondingly. Hybrid-hardware tradeoff: GPU/TPU mining of SHA-256 runs ~60× less efficient than dedicated ASICs (~1,000+ J/TH vs ~17 J/TH for Bitmain S21), so the hybrid wedge captures BTC value only because the GPU/TPU is *already in orbit* doing other work — a dedicated-ASIC orbital fleet would have better per-kWh mining cost but lose the compute-as-a-service flexibility. The ASIC-vs-hybrid-GPU tradeoff is a v0.8 falsification candidate. The model does not yet parameterize Bitcoin mining revenue (the workload-class table treats Bitcoin PoW as a 1.0× revenue multiplier on terrestrial DC pricing, which is a placeholder — a v0.8 model extension should add a separate `bitcoin_mining_revenue_per_kwh_orbital` parameter that tracks BTC price × emission / network hashrate × hardware-efficiency-class directly).
17. **Helio-chain-native space-colonization tokens: regulatory structuring + design.** The Phase 3-4 forward-looking option to issue helio-chain-native cryptocurrency or tokens (covered in §3.2) is real but legally uncertain. SEC enforcement under the Howey test treats most non-utility tokens as securities; cross-jurisdictional treatment (EU MiCA, UK FCA, Singapore MAS, etc.) varies materially. Mechanism design has fundamental tradeoffs: (a) PoW or PoS coin mined on helio-chain infrastructure (extends Bitcoin mode but adds market-adoption risk — most newly-issued PoW coins fail to gain liquidity); (b) utility token redeemable for in-orbit services (compute hours, beam-propulsion cargo capacity, reflector-service mission slots) — strongest "utility not security" Howey argument but requires service-pricing maturity that the helio chain won't have until Phase 2-3; (c) asset-backed security tied to orbital infrastructure depreciation cohorts — clearest legal structure (this IS a security) but requires registered offering / accredited-investor restrictions. The article does NOT propose a specific mechanism in v0.7. Open questions: when is the right launch window (Phase 3 typical / earlier / never)? Which mechanism survives regulatory scrutiny? Does the helio chain need a separate legal entity for the token issuance vs the operating company? These belong in the personal multi-decade roadmap (private doc), not in the public-facing v0.7 article.

These seventeen questions are deliberately enumerated rather than buried. The discipline is: an article that surfaces its own uncertainty is harder to dismiss than one that smooths it over.

## 6. Conclusion

The helio chain is a multi-decade civilization-level infrastructure bet. The bet is on four cost-and-capability curves bending favorably over 20-30 years, with launch cost as the single most-sensitive parameter. The parametric model exists; it produces specific numerical predictions; it includes explicit stalled-progress scenarios for each of the four curves; and it survives the adversarial-review traps that have killed prior orbital-power proposals — specifically by rejecting the central trap (SBSP-to-ground via microwave/laser beaming) that those proposals all fell into.

The article's keystone thesis, restated: **all electricity generated in orbit is consumed in orbit** — by orbital compute, in-space manufacturing, laser-based beam propulsion to Mars cargo, and (eventually) high-energy-physics infrastructure. The relevant cost comparison is helio-chain orbital-power vs Suncatcher-class self-launched-PV ($810/kW/yr at $200/kg per Suncatcher §2.4), NOT vs terrestrial renewables. At projected mid-2030s conditions with all four curves continuing per assumption, helio-chain orbital power costs $0.0091/kWh delivered to orbital consumers — roughly 10× below the Suncatcher self-launched-PV baseline (v0.11 numbers after engineering audit tightened conversion efficiency from 31.5% to a more realistic 24%). If launch cost plateaus at current $3,600/kg, orbital-power cost rises to $0.1635/kWh — comparable to current Starlink-class launched-power-price economics and above the Suncatcher baseline. Everything else (manufacturing, system mass, lifetime) matters less than launch cost. The bet reduces to a bet on Starship maturation.

Reflected SUNLIGHT to ground IS in scope: Phase 1 reflector services (premium illumination per mission + thermal warming + PV augmentation + greenhouse photons) deliver photons to ground targets via thin-film mirror, not electricity via microwave/laser beam. The 2026-05-25 premium-illumination falsification establishes Phase 1 launch-cost-tolerance is real (the only Phase that doesn't have to wait for Starship). Thermal warming was initially-claimed as a second validated Phase 1 wedge in v0.10 but was RETRACTED v0.11 after a thermodynamics audit caught a 230× dimensional overstatement (the Solspace 35 MWh/pass anchor is constellation-scale, not single-satellite); the physics-corrected falsification fails at all tested launch costs for single-satellite operations, and the wedge is recast as a Phase 2-3 research-portfolio item. PV augmentation + greenhouse photons remain PLACEHOLDER pending future falsification. **Phase 1 launch-cost-tolerance currently rests on premium illumination per mission alone** — a more fragile claim than v0.10 framed, and one of the article's most honest disclosures.

The article's framing decisions, restated: this is a forward-looking proposal, not advocacy and not dismissal. The model can be argued with on its inputs; the discipline is to provide tier-flagged anchors for every numeric claim and explicit ranges where uncertainty is honest. The kill case is documented honestly — if orbital compute never closes at any launch cost, the helio chain retains Phase 1 reflector services to ground (validated launch-cost-tolerant) + beam propulsion + orbital-power-as-a-service to other orbital customers as research-portfolio salvage paths. Direct-power-as-a-service to terrestrial rectennas via microwave/laser beam — the wedge that v0.4-v0.6 of the article contained and that the 2026-05-25 falsification showed doesn't close anyway — was definitively removed from the helio chain in v0.7 per scope clarification. SBSP-to-ground is the trap; the helio chain is what's left when you reject the trap.

The article does not predict whether the four curves will bend. It documents the conditions under which they would, and the consequences if they do not.

**What winning the bet looks like.** In a mid-2030s scenario where Starship matures on schedule and the four curves continue, the helio chain is operating: a Phase 1 reflector business — single-satellite spectacle illumination during the early years (event-spectacle / atmospheric effects / commercial brand experiences), scaling to constellation coordination (Reflect-Orbital-style 50K-mirror buildout) for defense ISR / SAR / disaster customer classes as the constellation matures; a Phase 2 hybrid orbital data center fleet running AI inference, scientific simulation, and rendering for external customers when demand is available — and mining Bitcoin on otherwise-idle GPU/TPU cycles, making every kWh of orbital power generate revenue at one rate or another; a Phase 3 in-space laser beam-propulsion infrastructure displacing chemical-rocket cost for cargo to Mars at a fraction of current per-kg pricing; and the beginnings of Phase 4 orbital high-energy-physics platforms that enable experiments terrestrial cooling and geographic constraints prohibit. Across all four phases, every kWh of generated electricity stays in orbit, doing useful work. The Znamya 2 spinning mirror from 1993 was the first hint that this was physics-possible; the helio chain is the parametric-economics case for why, three decades and one Starship trajectory later, it can also be economics-possible. None of this is guaranteed. The bet on Starship is real. The four-wedge research-portfolio salvage is real. The honest open questions in §5.9 are real. But the *shape* of the bet is now legible — and that legibility is what this article is for.
